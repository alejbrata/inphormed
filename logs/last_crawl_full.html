FormalPara Key Points

There is evidence, albeit weak, to support weight loss recommendations for patients with hidradenitis suppurativa to improve symptomatology, particularly if the patient is female and the body mass index is greater than 30.

Multiple studies show weak but significant evidence for recommending adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the minimization of sugar, dairy, and simple carbohydrates to improve hidradenitis suppurativa symptoms.

Testing and subsequent supplementation for vitamin D deficiency in patients with hidradenitis suppurativa and trialing zinc gluconate supplementation in patients resistant to standard hidradenitis suppurativa therapy have been shown in early studies to be noninvasive and inexpensive approaches to provide a small degree of clinical benefit.

1 Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition also known as acne inversa [1]. This disease can be associated with discomfort and disfigurement leading to social isolation, and a notable reduction in quality of life. It most commonly affects intertriginous regions of skin including the groin, perineal, inframammary, and axillary areas. The complete pathophysiology of HS is still unclear; however, lesion formation is thought to involve follicular hyperkeratosis within the folliculopilosebaceous units [2]. Hormonal changes stemming from diet, obesity, and androgen excess have been postulated to contribute to the follicular plugging and subsequent rupture and immune response associated with the genesis of HS [3]. In addition, sweat retention, friction, and larger intertriginous skin folds likely contribute to the exacerbation of this condition [3].

Multiple treatment modalities exist for managing this disease and include anti-androgens, topical and systemic antibiotics, biologics, immunosuppressive drugs, biguanides, fumarates, retinoids, surgical excision, and phototherapy [4]. For patients who struggle with HS, dietary, exercise, and weight loss interventions may be promising tools that can be made available to reduce the severity of this debilitating disease. We aim to answer the question of: how do the effects of dietary, weight loss, and/or exercise interventions affect the clinical course, severity, and/or subjective improvement of HS in adults over 18 years old? The goal of this review is to incorporate studies looking at diet, exercise, and weight loss interventions utilized for HS into a concise framework that can be used by clinicians to make evidence-based recommendations for patients struggling with this disease.

2 Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement was used to create this study. Case-control studies, cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials with five or more subjects conducted on adult participants (age > 18 years) were included. We excluded case reports, case series, and review papers. Eligible interventions were any type of exercise or weight loss modality intervention and any type of dietary intervention. Both interventions included changes to promote weight loss. Dietary interventions included removal of any triggering foods, addition of any protective foods, specific types of diets, dietary patterns, and the addition or negation of any vitamins or micronutrients. All forms of dietary intervention were included. Any dietary or exercise intervention evaluated in the context of purposeful concurrent medication use was excluded. Eligible methodology to measure changes in HS severity included Hurley staging, Sartorius score, the International Hidradenitis Suppurativa Severity Score System (IHS4), and subjective improvement.

We searched Scopus (1993 onwards), PubMed (1986 onwards), and MEDLINE (OVID interface, 1946 onwards). The initial search was conducted on 22 October, 2022. Search strategies were created by two reviewers in consultation with an academic librarian. These included hidradenitis suppurativa OR acne inversa and (diet OR dietary patterns OR dietary activities OR nutrition OR fruit OR vegetables OR gluten OR dairy OR sugars OR meat OR carbohydrates OR protein OR fats OR vitamin OR alcohol OR calorie OR weight loss OR obesity reduction) and hidradenitis suppurativa OR acne inversa and (exercise OR walking OR running OR jogging OR swimming OR resistance training OR weightlifting OR physical activity OR physical activities OR biking). Both searches were conducted on each database. Literature search results were exported to Cadima to remove duplicates and review articles.

There were 1384 studies screened and assessed for eligibility by two reviewers working independently. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewers’ decision. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 15 studies (nine cohort, five cross-sectional, and one cross-over) involving 2829 patients were selected for inclusion, these included 14 studies (2608 patients) looking at diet and one study (221 patients) looking at both diet and exercise (Fig. 1).

Table 1 summarizes the included studies’ findings and evidence levels according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine ratings [5]. Levels of evidence are defined as: level 1, randomized trials or systematic reviews of randomized trials, cross-sectional studies, inception cohort studies, or nested case-control studies; level 2, systematic review of surveys, randomized trials, individual cross-sectional studies, inception cohort studies, or (exceptional) observational studies with dramatic effect; level 3, cohort studies, local nonrandom sample, nonconsecutive studies, or studies without a consistently applied reference standard; level 4, case series, case–control study, or historically controlled studies; and level 5, mechanism-based reasoning. Level 1 represents evidence generally considered to be stronger and level 5 represents evidence generally considered to be weaker. The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing the risk of bias was used to evaluate each study [6].

3 Results

3.1 Weight Loss Interventions

The point prevalence of HS in obese patients is estimated to be as high as 18%, in addition, the level of obesity has been shown to correlate with disease severity. This raises questions about cause and effect [7, 8]. Studies performed by Macklis et al. and Kjaersgaard et al. support the rhetoric that general weight loss, especially among female individuals, those who are obese, and those who lose more than 50 pounds (22.7 kg), has a beneficial effect on subjective HS severity and Hurley stage [9, 10]. It is important to note that weight loss can also be a consequence of some types of dietary or exercise interventions and therefore these could act as confounding factors within these findings.

When looking specifically at bariatric surgery (BS) interventions, Kromann et al. demonstrated that substantial weight loss in the form of BS was associated with a resolution or reduction in the severity of HS symptoms in 68.6% of patients with prior self-reported HS based on a previously validated questionnaire [7]. However, an increase in active eruption sites in 11% of patients after BS suggests that the association between HS, BS, and weight is multi-factorial [8]. Garcovich et al. elucidated this point further by looking at patients with HS who had any past exposure to BS interventions and found that 83% of patients developed HS after BS using Hurley staging to classify severity [11]. Patients were also evaluated for micronutrient deficiencies after BS and were found to have significantly decreased serum zinc levels when compared with HS cases unrelated to BS [11]. These studies show how interventions presumed to positively affect the HS disease course can worsen the disease in a minority of individuals or potentially even cause the presentation of the disease itself in previously unaffected patients [7, 11].

Limitations such as confounding factors, response, and recall bias and the lack of a comparison group in any of these studies limit the level of evidence and the conclusions that can be drawn from the data. All reported studies in this section were rated as evidence level 3 by the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine ratings (Table 1).

3.2 Exacerbating and Alleviating Foods/Diets

Diet and specific food triggers are popular topics of discussion within the HS community. Multiple studies have been conducted to gauge the effectiveness of different dietary patterns and alleviating/exacerbating foods.

Studies by Dempsey et al., Fernandez et al., Kurzen and Kurzen, Lorite-Fuentes et al., Barrea et al., and Macklis et al. all evaluated the effect of different dietary patterns on HS severity [9, 12,13,14,15,16]. Commonalities across these studies included dairy, sweets, and processed grains being among the most common dietary triggers and a reduction in these food items was associated with subjective, Hurley stage, and IHS4 improvement [9, 12, 13]. Alleviating foods included vegetables, chicken, extra virgin olive oil, and fish [12, 13, 15]. Specific types of diets found to be beneficial for improving subjective HS severity were low-carbohydrate diets, Paleo diets, anti-inflammatory diets, and the Mediterranean diet [14,15,16]. The Mediterranean diet was particularly well studied with multiple studies elucidating a significant association between higher levels of objective adherence and lower Sartorius and IHS4 scores [15, 16]. These diets all share underlying commonalities, including the emphasis on the consumption of fruits and vegetables, and limiting processed foods, refined carbohydrates, and sugar [17].

Limitations of these studies include the cross-sectional survey design of the majority, which impedes the drawing of any type of causal inference. Because most studies were survey based and uncontrolled, there is a high likelihood of recall bias and confounding factors such as concurrent medication use impacting and interfering with results.

Recent reviews have also emphasized the positive effect that intermittent fasting can have on skin diseases [18]. Damiani et al. looked specifically at the effects of “Ramadan Fasting” (fasting every day from dawn to sunset) on HS severity and found a significant improvement in the number of abscesses and draining fistulas [19]. Importantly, 1 month after the termination of the fasting diet, only half of these patients maintained the beneficial effect of the fasting, suggesting that maintenance of this diet may be important in maintaining symptomatic remission [19].

Another prospective study by Cannistrà et al. studied patients with HS who tested positive for evidence of the anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antigen (ASCA) IgG with a cut-off for positivity of 10 U/mL, notably lower than the standard laboratory reference (40 U/mL) [20, 21]. These patients were placed on a diet excluding foods containing brewer’s or baker’s yeast (S. cerevisiae) and demonstrated stabilization of their clinical symptoms, regression of their skin lesions, and an immediate recurrence of skin lesions following accidental or voluntary consumption of brewer’s yeast and wheat [20]. A questionnaire was subsequently sent to these patients 6 years later and showed that the majority of patients who had followed this diet reported subjective HS symptom improvement without any other treatment [22]. However, the small sample size and the lack of controls limit the conclusions that can be drawn. All reported studies in this section were rated as evidence level 3 by the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine ratings (Table 1).

3.3 Micronutrient/Supplement Interventions

Multiple micronutrients have been evaluated to determine if the addition can assist in alleviating symptoms of HS. Two different studies by Brocard et al. and Molinelli et al. utilized 90 mg of zinc gluconate as an intervention for patients with HS [23, 24]. Both found significant improvements in Hurley stage, IHS4 score, and subjective severity with zinc supplementation [23, 24]. When zinc supplementation dose was reduced, relapses occurred, suggesting the supplementation of zinc may be more suppressive than curative [23]. None of the patients experienced deterioration of their HS during the zinc treatment but a minority experienced side effects including diarrhea, abdominal distention, nausea, and esophagitis [23, 24]. Important limitations of these studies include a low Hurley stage of patients and previous treatment with minocycline [23, 24].

One study by Fabbrocini et al. analyzed the effect of oral vitamin D supplementation on patients with HS resistant to standard therapy [25]. Ninety percent of patients with HS in this cohort had deficient or insufficient levels of vitamin D at baseline. Of note, a negative correlation was also elucidated between vitamin D levels and the severity of HS [25]. Patients received oral supplementation of vitamin D until levels became sufficient according to US Endocrine Society guidelines. After 6 months, 67.5% of patients observed a significant improvement in their Sartorius score of more than 20 points [25]. However, the small sample size and lack of a follow-up restrict the ability to draw concrete conclusions about the efficacy of long-term vitamin D supplementation in HS. All reported studies in this section were rated as evidence level 3 by the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine ratings (Table 1).

4 Discussion

Overall, more evidence is needed to elucidate the relationship between HS and diet, weight loss, and exercise modalities. An important weakness identified by this review is the lack of evidence for any exercise-based intervention on HS. A recent study by Lorite-Fuentes et al. identifies that both self-reported disease activity and IHS4 scores showed an inverse association with the intensity of activity, but neither finding was statistically significant [15]. A recent review of exercise and dermatologic conditions identified that regular exercise has many beneficial effects on skin health including reducing the thinning of the stratum corneum, decreasing the aging of skin cells, and reducing the risk of psoriasis [26]. Studies looking directly at the effect of exercise on HS would be useful to guide management by clinicians when deciding to recommend weight loss interventions using diet, exercise, or both and to determine which has a more significant impact on disease progression. Importantly, controlled studies evaluating different exercise modalities effects on HS could provide insightful conclusions about whether the physiologic benefits of exercise outweigh the potential for HS exacerbation that could be precipitated by increased friction and perspiration that occur when engaging in physical activity.

Weight reduction is an important intervention currently utilized by some clinicians in the management of HS. Current literature supports this intervention, especially when treating female individuals, obese patients, and in those who lose more than 50 pounds (22.7 kg) [9]. However, there are details that must be further studied. One of these includes the onset of HS for some patients after BS, specifically malabsorptive procedures [11]. Many relevant mechanisms could be attributed to this association, including the role of micronutrient deficiencies (zinc, iron, vitamin D, vitamin A) on innate immune responses and follicular hyperkeratosis; adipokine dysregulation, the disturbance of the gut microbiome; and the modification of the perifollicular, dermal extracellular matrix and augmented skin friction that can occur with excess skin and contour deformities [11]. This is an interesting phenomenon that is alluded to by several studies that show an increased prevalence of dietary insufficiencies in HS and how supplementation can assist in improving the disease process [11, 25].

Bariatric surgery has also been shown to have a positive impact on HS disease progression, where significant improvements in HS symptoms were seen after subsequent BS [7]. Clearly, these conflicting studies show that more evidence is needed to elucidate the relationship between BS and HS and how it plays a role in disease progression and onset.

Dietary restriction and specific dietary components have also been emphasized by both clinicians and more recently in the online HS community. Evidence for diets such as the Mediterranean diet that minimize sugar, highly processed carbohydrates, and dairy and emphasize fruits, vegetables, and chicken seem to have beneficial effects for patients with HS in early studies [9, 12,13,14,15,16]. These dietary findings are all in line with the current understanding of the pathophysiology underlying HS. The clinical symptoms associated with HS are downstream effects of a rupture of the folliculopilosebaceous unit. Two key ingredients of the ‘Western’ diet, highly refined simple carbohydrates and dairy products, appear to mediate the initial plugging of the duct [27]. Dairy contains three components that could potentially drive this process including: (1) casein, which may elevate insulin-like growth factor-1; (2) simple carbohydrates and whey, which increase insulin levels; and (3) 5-alpha-reduced hormone, a product of bovine mammary glands [28]. Insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1 open the androgen receptor, increasing its availability to androgens from numerous sources and contributing to the plugging of the folliculopilosebaceous unit [28].

However, major limitations exist within these studies, including poor evidence level ratings owing to the cross-sectional survey-based nature of the majority. High-quality randomized controlled trials evaluating different diet subtypes and specific food group effects on HS are needed to determine the ideal dietary recommendations for patients.

Other diets have also had success in certain subgroups of patients with HS, including the exclusion diet for brewer’s yeast (in those who tested positive for anti-ASCA IgG), which correlates to a diet that does not allow any bakery products, potentially mimicking a lower carbohydrate/minimally processed food diet [20, 22]. ASCA is most notable for its role in the diagnosis of Crohn’s disease, but it is also found in various other autoimmune diseases, such as Behçet’s disease, autoimmune thyroid diseases, and autoimmune hepatitis [22]. This study emphasizes the need for more research regarding diet and anti-ASCA IgG testing in order to guide management and dietary recommendations for patients with HS. A small study also showed that Ramadan fasting has promise for reducing HS severity but larger controlled studies are needed to look specifically at this pattern of eating to determine its applicability to a wider patient population [19].

When looking specifically at dietary supplements, it is possible that vitamin D plays a role in the pathophysiologic mechanism of HS. Numerous studies have shown a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in patients with HS and its supplementation has been shown to have beneficial effects on HS severity with minimal side effects [25, 29, 30]. The biologically active metabolite of vitamin D3, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3, does not only play a role in controlling bone and calcium metabolism but has also proven to be essential in immunomodulation via the nuclear vitamin D receptor found on activated T cells and antigen-presenting cells [31].

Immunomodulation by 1,25(OH)2D3 has been demonstrated in vivo in numerous autoimmune diseases [26]. Recent studies elucidate that vitamin D deficiency is more common in atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, and that disease severity decreases with supplementation [25]. This suggests vitamin D status could begin to play a role in the assessment and management of HS, though more studies are needed to define the parameters around which patients would benefit most.

Zinc has also shown promise in ameliorating symptoms of HS. Ninety milligrams of zinc gluconate has shown at least a partially suppressive effect on low Hurley stage HS and while not curative, this suggests a role for zinc supplementation in patients with this disease. These positive responses to zinc in HS could be related to the anti-inflammatory activity of zinc salts. In patients with acne who were treated with zinc salts, it was shown to inhibit the chemotaxis of polynuclear neutrophils [32]. Zinc also alters the production of interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, two pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by keratinocytes [33]. Additionally, it possesses anti-androgen activity, with studies showing it effects on 5-alpha-reductase type I and II expression [23].

It is essential to recognize that major limitations surround the current data on HS and weight loss, diet, and exercise. The small sample sizes, lack of control groups, and retrospective cross-sectional survey-based nature of the majority of studies included in this review make it difficult to draw concrete conclusions based on current data. Large diet-based randomized controlled trials are notoriously difficult to perform but should be considered based on the promising data regarding diet-based and micronutrient interventions utilized for HS that are reported in this article.

5 Conclusions

It is difficult to evaluate the efficacy of individual dietary interventions because of confounding factors such as concurrent medication regimens and weight loss that could be associated with many diets. It is also difficult to validate the adherence to any single diet, further complicated by the small sample size and the lack of controls present in many of the studies discussed. However, with the acknowledgment of these hurdles, the literature does show evidence that diet may play a role in ameliorating symptoms of HS. Concrete recommendations that can be given to patients with some level of confidence include encouraging weight loss for symptom improvement, particularly if the patient is female and the BMI is greater than 30, encouraging strong adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and minimizing sugar, dairy, and simple carbohydrates. In addition, testing for vitamin D deficiency in all patients with HS and trialing zinc gluconate supplementation in patients resistant to standard therapies are noninvasive and inexpensive approaches to provide adjunctive benefit to the provider’s standard treatment regimen. Clinicians and researchers should strive to further define these relationships so that we may improve the outcomes of patients struggling with this debilitating disease.
